\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{subsec:data}

The training corpus consists of 6,511 frontal catalogue photographs of
eyeglass frames, the same collection used in our earlier work on joint
image--mask synthesis~\cite{shubitidze2026masks}. Each source image is square,
between roughly 1350 and 1650 pixels on a side with a median of 1463, and
shows a single unworn frame, roughly centred, against a near-uniform light
background. Images are centre-cropped
to a 2:1 aspect ratio and resized to $256 \times 512$ pixels (height $\times$
width), which follows the natural proportions of a frame viewed from the
front and avoids the padding a square canvas would demand.

Two properties of this corpus matter for what follows. The first is the
background. Because it is close to uniform, the frame silhouette can be found
by thresholding on the distance from the background colour. No learned
segmentation is needed, and the generator does not have to produce a mask.
All geometric and symmetry measures in Section~\ref{sec:metrics} use
silhouettes obtained this way. The second property is size. At 6,511 images
the corpus is small for adversarial training at this resolution, and several
of the failures reported below were caused by the discriminator memorising
the training set.

Two dataset-level interventions are used throughout. Horizontal flips are
applied to the dataset rather than as a stochastic augmentation, doubling the
effective corpus to roughly 13,000 images. Sampling is additionally weighted
by inverse frequency over a $16 \times 16$ histogram of each frame's mean
CIELAB $a^*b^*$ chromaticity, with exponent 0.5 and a weight cap of 4. The reason for this weighting and its effect are reported in
Section~\ref{subsec:ablations}.

\subsection{Base Model and Stabilisation}
\label{subsec:base}

The starting point is the conditional StyleGAN2 variant of our previous
work~\cite{shubitidze2026masks}, which generated an RGB image and a binary
frame mask from a single forward pass and reached an FID of 6.13. A later
revision of that model added an eight-layer mapping network, adaptive
discriminator augmentation~\cite{karras2020ada}, path-length regularisation, a
feature-matching term and KID-based checkpoint selection. It produced sharper samples and the best domain metrics of that work.
However, its FID rose to 38.47, and its colour-diversity score fell to 0.0567,
against 0.0995 for the earlier run.

This comparison also shows why we do not reuse the earlier diversity
metrics. The earlier colour-diversity score is a mean pairwise distance
computed on the generated images only. On the real corpus it is 0.0338, so
both generators scored far above the real data. A score of this kind rewards
spread, but it does not check whether the spread matches the real
distribution, and a generator can raise it by producing colours the catalogue
does not contain. The metrics of Section~\ref{sec:metrics} always compare
against the real set.

Removing the mask branch, as the present scope requires, destabilised training
badly, and three defects had to be corrected before a stable unconditional
baseline existed. The first was the output head. The generator projected to
RGB through a $\tanh$ nonlinearity, inherited from the mask-supervised model.
Approximately 86\% of the pixels in a cropped and resized catalogue
image are near-white background, so every loss term drives the large majority
of pixels toward $+1$, where $\tanh$ has vanishing gradient. We adopted the linear RGB head of the official StyleGAN2
generator~\cite{karras2020stylegan2}.

The second defect was in the mapping network, and it is the one with a
plausible bearing on the low colour diversity of the predecessor run. Eight
stacked default linear layers with LeakyReLU activations contract the signal
at every step, because standard initialisation scales weights by the inverse
square root of the fan-in without the equalized-learning-rate correction.
We measured this at initialisation on a batch of 4096 standard-normal
latents. The style codes have a standard deviation of 0.0184, against 1.0000
for the latents. Most of the loss happens early: the standard deviation is
0.583 after the first linear layer, 0.052 after the fourth, and about 0.028
after that. A style code of that
dispersion carries almost no per-image information, which leaves per-pixel
noise injection as the dominant source of variation between samples. Replacing
the stack with equalized-learning-rate linear layers~\cite{karras2018progressive}
at a learning-rate multiplier of 0.01, as in the StyleGAN mapping
network~\cite{karras2019stylegan}, raises the
style-code standard deviation to 1.0968 under the identical measurement.

The third defect was the adversarial loss. The hinge
formulation~\cite{lim2017geometric} produces no gradient once real and fake
scores separate beyond its margins. Under mask supervision the discriminator
had never built margins wide enough for this to matter; without it, the two
distributions separate within a few epochs, the discriminator loss reaches
exactly zero, and training stalls. Substituting the non-saturating logistic loss~\cite{goodfellow2014gan},
the standard choice in StyleGAN2-ADA~\cite{karras2020stylegan2,karras2020ada},
removed the stall.
With the three corrections in place training was stable; we did not measure
their contributions separately.

\subsection{Training Configuration}
\label{subsec:training}

All models reported here are derived from
StyleGAN2-ADA~\cite{karras2020stylegan2,karras2020ada} and implemented in stock
PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch}, with no compiled custom operators. The
generator maps a 512-dimensional latent through the eight-layer
equalized-learning-rate mapping network described above to a 512-dimensional
style code, and synthesises through six weight-demodulated stages from $8
\times 16$ to $256 \times 512$ with skip-RGB connections and a linear output
head. A self-attention module~\cite{zhang2019self} is placed at $32 \times 64$
to give the network access to long-range structure, such as the
correspondence between the left and right halves of the frame. Channel widths follow $512 \rightarrow
256 \rightarrow 256 \rightarrow 128 \rightarrow 64 \rightarrow 32 \rightarrow
16$, giving 8.50~M generator parameters; the discriminator uses $16
\rightarrow 32 \rightarrow 64 \rightarrow 128 \rightarrow 256 \rightarrow 256$
for 2.50~M parameters. Both width multipliers were varied, and the outcome is
reported in Section~\ref{subsec:negative}.

Training uses the non-saturating logistic loss with a lazy $R_1$ gradient
penalty~\cite{mescheder2018converge} at $\gamma = 5$, applied every 16 steps.
Path-length regularisation~\cite{karras2020stylegan2} uses weight 4.0 on the
same interval, with a batch of 16. The generator also receives a
feature-matching term~\cite{salimans2016improved} at weight 2.0 with random
real--fake pairing, and a VGG perceptual term~\cite{simonyan2015vgg} at
weight 0.75 in which each generated image is paired with its nearest real
image in the batch. The discriminator applies a group-wise
minibatch standard deviation layer~\cite{karras2018progressive} over groups of
four and blurs feature maps before average pooling. Adaptive discriminator
augmentation uses horizontal flips, integer-pixel translation, brightness
jitter, additive noise and cutout, with the probability ceiling at 0.95; hue
and saturation augmentation is disabled, for reasons given in
Section~\ref{subsec:ablations}. Both networks use Adam~\cite{kingma2015adam} with $\beta_1 = 0$ and
$\beta_2 = 0.99$, at learning rates of $2 \times 10^{-4}$ for the generator and
$5 \times 10^{-5}$ for the discriminator. Style mixing~\cite{karras2019stylegan}
is applied with probability 0.9. An exponential moving average of the
generator weights~\cite{karras2018progressive}, with decay 0.999, is used for
all sampling and evaluation. The batch size is 16. On a single RTX~4090
with 24~GB of memory an epoch takes approximately 180 to 190 seconds.

\subsection{Generation Protocol}
\label{subsec:generation}

Sampling has its own design choices, independent of training, and we swept them separately. We varied five things. The first is the truncation coefficient $\psi$. The
second is layered truncation, where the coarse and fine synthesis stages get
different coefficients. The third is whether each image comes from a single
latent or from a blend of several style codes. The fourth is truncation
toward $k$ cluster centres instead of the global style mean. The fifth is
rejection sampling, either by manifold precision or by the artifact suite of
Section~\ref{subsec:artifacts}. Nine protocols built from
these levers were evaluated on a common checkpoint
(Table~\ref{tab:protocols}), each generating 10,000 images at a fixed seed.

Three findings follow from this sweep, and one of them runs against common
practice. In this domain, truncation below $\psi = 1$ is not a trade-off but a plain
loss. Moving from 0.85 to 1.0 to 1.2 improves FID and every diversity measure
at the same time. The low-$\psi$ settings normally used to improve image
quality~\cite{brock2019biggan,karras2019stylegan} are therefore not on the
front at all.
Precision does rise as $\psi$ falls, but the density column shows that this
is not better fidelity. At $\psi = 0.85$ density reaches 2.03, which means
the samples crowd into the densest regions of the real distribution. FID,
which accounts for both fidelity and coverage, gets worse.
Synthesising each image from a single latent beats blending several style
codes at matched $\psi$ on nearly every axis. The reason is simple:
averaging style codes pulls every sample toward the population mean. The
blended scheme was nevertheless the default we inherited from our previous
work.
Layered truncation gives the best compromise. It truncates only the three
coarse stages, which control silhouette and proportions, and leaves the
three fine stages, which control colour and texture, untruncated. This gives
the lowest FID at full yield. It also has higher colour coverage than the
filtered $\psi = 1.2$ set, because the stages that control colour are not
truncated.

Two levers brought no benefit. Truncation toward eight centres differs from the single-centre layered
protocol by 0.02 FID, which is within the measurement noise of
Section~\ref{subsec:noise}. At $\psi \geq 1$ truncation is too weak for the
choice of centre to matter. A precision filter added to the layered protocol raised precision but
lowered recall and colour coverage, so it moved along the trade-off rather
than improving it. Filtering is examined further in
Section~\ref{subsec:filtering}.

The layered protocol was then fixed and used for every model comparison in
this paper. It uses $\psi = 1.0$ for the three coarse stages and $\psi = 1.2$
for the three fine stages, a single latent per image, 10,000 images, and
generation seed 42. Because the protocol was selected on an intermediate checkpoint with FID
10.2, we repeated the comparison on the two final models
(Section~\ref{subsec:transfer}).

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{subsec:reproducibility}

Bitwise reproduction of these runs is not achievable and is not claimed.
cuDNN autotuning and non-deterministic GPU kernels were not disabled. Also,
each resumed run re-seeds the random number generator instead of restoring
its state, so the batch order after a resume differs from that of an
uninterrupted run.
What we provide is the code, the configurations and the trained weights of
the two final models, with fixed seeds. Run-to-run variation under a
different training seed was not measured. This would require repeating a
full training run, roughly 60 GPU-hours for one model.

Long runs were trained in several resumed segments. The 1,600-epoch sharp1
run, for example, consists of six segments of 300, 200, 300, 357, 43 and 400
epochs, 83.9 hours in total. Epochs are reported as the number of epochs
completed; the checkpoint files store a zero-based index one lower.

\section{A Diversity Metric Suite for Eyeglass Frames}
\label{sec:metrics}

The suite follows three design rules. First, every measure is computed
from the images alone: frame silhouettes are recovered by background
thresholding, so no generated masks and no learned segmentation are
involved, and the same code evaluates real and generated sets identically.
Second, no diversity statistic is reported without its value on the real
corpus. Section~\ref{subsec:base} showed what happens otherwise: a spread
statistic with no real reference can be improved indefinitely by drifting
off-distribution, and the predecessor metrics rewarded exactly that.
Third, each new metric is tested on synthetic images with a known answer
before it is used. Section~\ref{subsec:aligned_sym} shows why this matters:
a symmetry score can respond more strongly to camera pose than to the defect
it is meant to measure.

\subsection{Geometry, Colour, Feature-Space and Perceptual Measures}
\label{subsec:metric_families}

Silhouettes are extracted by taking the median colour of the border pixels
as the background, marking as foreground every pixel whose RGB distance from
it exceeds 30, and removing specks with a $3 \times 3$ morphological opening.
This extraction did not fail on any of the 6,511 real images.

Geometric diversity is described by nine values per silhouette. Two are
areas: the fraction of the image covered by the silhouette, and the fraction
covered after filling its holes, which reflects overall frame size. Two
describe the bounding box: its aspect ratio and its height relative to the
image. Mean and maximum rim thickness, from the distance transform, separate
thin wire frames from thick acetate ones. Hollowness, one minus the ratio of
silhouette area to filled area, measures how open the lenses are. The last
two are the number of enclosed openings, which approximates the lens count,
and solidity, the filled area divided by its convex hull. For each descriptor the generated
set is compared with the real set twice: as a dispersion ratio (standard
deviations, supported by the 5th-to-95th percentile range) and as a
Jensen--Shannon distance between the two histograms. The ratio asks
whether the generator spans as much of the range as the catalogue; the
distance asks whether it occupies that range in the same proportions.

Colour is measured on frame pixels only, in CIELAB. Three statistics are
reported. The mean pairwise distance between per-image mean frame colours
measures palette spread. The entropy of a chroma-weighted hue histogram
over chromatic pixels, normalised to $[0, 1]$, distinguishes broad hue
coverage from a few well-separated colour clusters, a pattern that the
pairwise distance alone cannot see. Colour-gamut coverage
(\emph{ab~coverage}) is the fraction of occupied cells in a $16 \times 16$
histogram of per-image mean colour over $a^*, b^* \in [-40, 40]$.
Occupancy counts grow with sample size, so this quantity is only ever
compared between sets of equal size; all values in this paper use matched
5,000-image subsamples. The bin count is a free parameter, and we verified
that the ordering of the sets compared in this paper is unchanged at
$12 \times 12$, $16 \times 16$ and $20 \times 20$ resolution. A generated
set can exceed the real coverage by placing frame colours in cells where
the catalogue has no examples; we therefore read ab~coverage as a distance
to the real value, not as a score to maximise.

Feature-space measures use Inception-v3 pool3
embeddings~\cite{szegedy2016inception}. FID~\cite{heusel2017fid} and KID~\cite{binkowski2018kid} (subset size 1,000)
are computed on the same 5,000-image subsamples and serve as quality
references. They are comparable between the experiments in this paper, but
not with values reported on other datasets. Improved
precision and recall~\cite{kynkaanniemi2019precision} and density and
coverage~\cite{naeem2020prdc} are computed with $k = 5$ nearest
neighbours; recall and coverage are the primary distribution-level
diversity measures. The Vendi score~\cite{friedman2023vendi} is computed
on 2,000-image subsets; because Inception features spread very little on
a single narrow product category, its absolute value is small even for
the real set, and we report only the generated-to-real ratio. A
memorisation check compares the mean distance from each generated image
to its nearest real neighbour with the same statistic between real images;
a ratio well below one flags copying. Finally, perceptual diversity is the mean pairwise
LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018lpips}, VGG variant over 500 random pairs, within the
generated set, reported against the same statistic within the real set.

\subsection{Pose-Aligned Mirror Symmetry}
\label{subsec:aligned_sym}

Eyeglass frames are bilaterally symmetric objects, so a symmetry defect is
a structural failure worth measuring. The obvious construction, and the
one we used first, mirrors the silhouette's boundary band about
the vertical centre line of its bounding box and scores the overlap. That
axis, however, is the frame's true symmetry axis only when the photograph
is perfectly fronto-parallel, and catalogue photographs are not: measured
on the real set, 42\% of images need an axis correction of three or
more pixels. The fixed-axis score therefore conflates photographic pose
with shape.

The pose-aligned version searches over horizontal axis shifts of up to
$\pm 8$ pixels and rotations of up to $\pm 3$ degrees, and keeps the best
boundary-band intersection-over-union. Table~\ref{tab:symval} shows the
validation on synthetic silhouettes with known ground truth. A tilt of two degrees costs a perfectly symmetric frame
0.64 of the fixed-axis score but only 0.007 of the aligned score, while a
genuine asymmetry, one temple foreshortened by a quarter, costs 0.57 fixed
and 0.40 aligned. Alignment removes 98.9\% of the pose penalty and
retains 69.8\% of the shape penalty: the aligned score responds to
shape, the fixed score mostly to pose. The search costs about 29~ms per
image on a desktop CPU, roughly five minutes for a 10,000-image set. One
validity condition emerged from the same test: the implementation pads the
bounding box by 16 pixels and clips at the canvas edge, so silhouettes
closer than that to the border are measured with a biased axis. Catalogue
framing leaves margins several times wider, but the condition is stated
here because the probe found it.

\subsection{Mirror-Axis Offset Dispersion}
\label{subsec:yaw}

The axis search of the previous subsection also gives a diversity measure:
the offset of the best mirror axis from the bounding-box centre. Table~\ref{tab:symval}
establishes what it responds to. Moving a symmetric silhouette by $+12$ or $-20$ pixels leaves the offset at
exactly zero, because the search is centred on the bounding box. The offset
therefore does not measure where the frame sits in the image. What
moves it is differential temple foreshortening: shortening one temple by
10\% shifts the recovered axis by 5 pixels, by 25\% by 8
pixels. This is what a small rotation of the frame about the vertical axis (yaw)
does to a frontal photograph, so the offset measures the variety of viewing
angles the generator reproduces. The two measures complement each other: at 10\% foreshortening the aligned score barely moves
(0.973 against 0.986 for the symmetric case) while the offset already
reads 5 pixels, so mild yaw is nearly invisible to the symmetry score and
plainly visible to the offset.

The set-level statistic reports the standard deviation and interquartile
range of the offset, the fraction of images with an offset of three or
more pixels, and the ratio of the generated standard deviation to the
real one. A ratio well below one means the generator pins every frame
into a canonically frontal pose, a collapse of pose variety that, as
Section~\ref{subsec:ablations} shows, none of FID, KID, LPIPS or
ab~coverage detects.

\subsection{Realistic Novelty Rate}
\label{subsec:novelty}

Recall and coverage measure how much of the catalogue the generator
reproduces. For dataset augmentation the complementary question matters as
much: does the generator produce frames that exist nowhere in the
catalogue, and are those frames plausible designs rather than artifacts?
The suite answers it with a calibrated nearest-neighbour construction in
the same Inception feature space as the other measures. The reference set
contains every real image together with its horizontal mirror, since
training saw dataset-level flips and a mirrored copy of a catalogue frame
is not a new design. The novelty threshold is set from the real set. For each real image we
compute the distance to its nearest other reference image, excluding its own
mirror. The threshold $\tau$ is the 95th percentile of these distances, so
only 5\% of real images are this far from the rest of the catalogue. A generated
image is novel if its nearest-reference distance exceeds $\tau$, and
realistic if it additionally lies inside the $k$-nearest-neighbour feature
manifold of the reference set, the improved-precision
criterion~\cite{kynkaanniemi2019precision}. The realistic novelty rate is
the fraction of generated images satisfying both.

Two checks calibrate the construction. Feeding the real subsample back in
as the generated set yields a novelty rate of exactly zero, so copies can
never count as novel. Scoring one disjoint half of the real set against
the other, where exchangeability predicts a rate near the designed 5\%, yields 4.4\%. One caveat concerns comparability: the realism
criterion is evaluated against the flip-augmented reference, whose denser
$k$-nearest-neighbour manifold is stricter than the precision reported in
the main results, and the two numbers are not comparable.

The manifold test is weakest where novelty is highest. A
$k$-nearest-neighbour ball is larger where real samples are sparse, so the
realism test is most permissive in exactly the sparse regions where novel
samples are found. For this corpus these are mostly rimless and thin-wire
designs, which are also the hardest frames to render. The suite therefore
also reports a strict rate: the fraction of generated images that are novel,
inside the manifold, and pass every check of the artifact suite of
Section~\ref{subsec:artifacts}. The script also saves each novel image next
to its nearest catalogue image, so the results can be checked visually.

\subsection{Structural Artifact Suite}
\label{subsec:artifacts}

Distribution-level metrics do not show whether individual images have
visible defects. The suite therefore includes twelve per-image checks: silhouette fragment count, enclosed-opening count, silhouette
mirror overlap, lens-shape mismatch, speckle fraction, interior sharpness,
rim breaks, rim contrast, contour wobble, the fixed-axis and pose-aligned
edge-symmetry scores, and an appearance-symmetry check, the mean CIELAB
difference between frame pixels and their mirrored counterparts. Each check uses the 2nd or 98th percentile of the real corpus as its
threshold, so about 2\% of real images fail any single check. A generated
set is described by its flag rate for each check, compared with the real
rate, and by its clean rate, the fraction of images with no flags.

Two caveats govern interpretation. The checks are one-sided: they flag
too little sharpness or too many fragments, but nothing flags an image
for being implausibly clean, so a generator that collapses onto safe,
canonical configurations can score better than real. A clean rate above
the real corpus is treated in this paper as a warning sign to be
investigated, not as a success. And the checks inherit the limits of
silhouette extraction: on thin-wire, rimless and translucent frames the
thresholded silhouette is itself unstable, so part of any flag-rate gap
on those classes is measurement noise rather than generation failure.

The $R_1$ sweep brackets the discriminator regularisation optimum from both
sides. At $\gamma = 2$ the discriminator overfits, and training KID reaches its
minimum at epoch 115. At $\gamma = 5$ the minimum is almost three times lower
and comes at epoch 190. At $\gamma = 10$ training is stable but slow: the
minimum comes only at epoch 370 and is more than twice the $\gamma = 5$
value. The interesting result is on the diversity side. If a weaker
discriminator allowed the generator more freedom, $\gamma = 10$ should be
the most diverse setting. It is the least: against $\gamma = 5$ it loses on
FID (22.20 versus 13.62), recall (0.029 versus 0.104), colour coverage
(0.211 versus 0.305) and coverage (0.451 versus 0.690), every gap far
beyond the noise floor. Diversity did not come from weakening the
discriminator but from a discriminator strong enough to demand coverage
without memorising.

The colour-coverage gap of the mid-project models was a rare-mode
problem: the missing $a^*b^*$ histogram cells contained only
153 of the 6,511 real images (2.35\%), dominated by purple and violet
acetate frames. A sampling change was therefore enough. Inverse-frequency
oversampling (Section~\ref{subsec:data}), which makes boosted images 3.7\% of
training draws, raised colour coverage from 0.422 to 0.566 while FID improved
from 8.90 to 7.80. The two runs, ppl4 and rebalance2, differ only in the
sampling weights, although their selected checkpoints are from epochs 310 and
460. An earlier, stronger version of the same idea, a fixed $\times 8$ or
$\times 4$ weight on images in the missing and under-represented cells, overshot
to 0.684, above the real value of 0.566, at FID 18.60. Coverage increases
with the strength of the oversampling, and too much of it overshoots. One confound must be recorded: the overshoot run also used a
lower ADA probability ceiling (0.85 instead of 0.95), so its FID is not a
clean measurement of the oversampling dose alone. Its coverage overshoot,
the point the comparison rests on, does not depend on that.

Hue and saturation augmentation, the colour half of ADA, consistently cost
colour diversity rather than adding it. The clean same-era comparison is
divcfg2 (colour ADA on) against nocolorada (off): coverage 0.168 against
0.367 at an equal 400-epoch budget. The late-era accident repeats it at
larger scale: rebalance3 shipped with colour ADA unintentionally re-enabled
and reached coverage 0.176, and rebalance4, identical apart from the
restored flag and 200 further epochs, reached 0.613. The missing-cell analysis shows where the colours were lost. under rebalance3 111 histogram cells holding
928 real images (14.25\%) were missing, all of them at a chroma of 7.9 or more, while the
near-neutral cells were all covered; under rebalance4 the misses fell to 9
cells holding 10 images (0.15\%). We explain this with the augmentation leaking described for
ADA~\cite{karras2020ada}. The augmentation probability stayed at its upper
limit, so hue jitter was applied at almost every step. The discriminator
then became insensitive to colour and no longer constrained the generator's
colour distribution.

Blurring discriminator feature maps before pooling changed the failure mode
of long training runs. Without it, the mean discriminator margin of rebalance2 grew from 4.5 to
37.5 over epochs 400 to 600. Over the same period its training KID became 25
times worse (0.0021 at epoch 460, 0.052 at epoch 595), and the recall of its
final checkpoint fell to zero. We read this as the discriminator memorising
the 6,511 real images, with the generator drifting away from the data
distribution. With blurpool, rebalance4's margin
over the same windows grew only from 1.9 to 4.4, and was 7.2 over epochs 700
to 744; no runaway occurred in 800 epochs. Blurpool was introduced together
with dataset x-flips and the group minibatch standard deviation layer, so
this comparison does not isolate it. We attribute the change mainly to the
blur because it is the only one of the three that limits the
high-frequency detail available to the discriminator.

Mirror coupling is the only architecture change used in a headline model,
and both its benefit and its cost are informative. Frames are bilaterally
symmetric objects, and plain training leaves a residual shape-asymmetry
gap: the pose-aligned symmetry median of sharp1 at 1,600 epochs is 0.059
below the real value. The mirror1 variant gives each position of the
generator access to the horizontally flipped feature map at the $32 \times 64$
and $64 \times 128$ stages. It closes the gap and overshoots it (0.016 above
real), but it loses diversity in two ways. Its colour coverage drops to
0.523, against 0.629 for the sharp1 checkpoint with the same training length.
Its yaw dispersion falls to 0.44 of the real value: only 5.3\% of its frames
have an axis offset of 3 pixels or more, against 41.9\% of real frames. Its
appearance-symmetry flag rate of 0.30\%, against 2.01\% for real images, is
a score better than real, which Section~\ref{subsec:artifacts} treats as a
warning sign: the model over-mirrors. The mirror2 variant mirrors about a learned axis for each sample instead of
the fixed centre. It recovers most of what mirror1 lost. It reaches the best
recall of any run (0.705) and colour coverage of 0.613. Its aligned symmetry
gap is only $-0.017$, and its appearance-symmetry flag rate of 1.06\% is
closer to the real value. What
it does not recover is yaw: its dispersion ratio is 0.45, essentially
mirror1's 0.44. Both axis parameterisations forbid differential temple
foreshortening, which is consistent with yaw collapse being a property of
the mirror prior itself rather than of the axis choice. Plain training
preserves it (sharp1: 1.07). FID, KID, LPIPS and colour coverage do not
measure pose, so this loss is visible only through the offset dispersion.

\subsection{Rejection Filtering as a Pareto Knob}
\label{subsec:filtering}

Post-hoc rejection of low-quality samples is an established way to trade
yield for quality~\cite{azadi2019drs}; here the artifact suite provides the
rejection rule, and the question is what filtering costs. We tested this on rebalance2, the last model that needed it, with three
settings: no filter, at most one flag, and zero flags. FID went from 7.802 to
7.956 to 8.566, precision from 0.881 to 0.892 to 0.890, and colour coverage
from 0.566 to 0.547 to 0.520. Filtering buys structural cleanliness and pays in colour,
because the structural defects concentrate in the rare-colour tail of the
distribution, so rejecting flagged images preferentially rejects rare
colours. It is a movement along a front, not an improvement of it.

From rebalance4 onward the unfiltered clean rate is at the level of the
real corpus (86.4\% against 86.7\%). The final models therefore use no
filtering, and all their results are measured on unfiltered output.

\section{Checkpoint Selection Under a Diversity Objective}
\label{sec:checkpoints}

Every model in this paper was represented by the checkpoint with the lowest
training KID, which is common practice. KID is a single distribution-level
score, and the sharp1 trajectory of Section~\ref{subsec:pareto} already
showed FID improving while recall and colour coverage declined in the same
run. This raises a direct question: does the minimum-KID checkpoint also
give the best dataset? We tested it in two ways.

\subsection{Best Versus Last Checkpoint}
\label{subsec:best_last}

For each run, the minimum-KID checkpoint (best) and the final checkpoint
(last) were generated and evaluated under the identical protocol. Two runs
were excluded: sg2f, whose checkpoint no longer computes the function it was
trained as (Section~\ref{subsec:silent}), and edgeD, whose best and last
checkpoints contain bit-identical EMA generator weights. The remaining sixteen
runs were grouped by comparing best and last on FID and recall, each against
its noise floor; differences inside the floor count as ties
(Table~\ref{tab:bestlast}).

In five runs the best checkpoint wins on both axes. Three of these are late
collapses: ppl4, rebalance2 and wide2 lose almost all recall by their final
epoch, and rebalance2's FID rises from 7.87 to 71.13. In these runs KID
selection did exactly what it should and saved a usable model. In five more
runs the best checkpoint is better on FID while recall does not differ
beyond noise.

The other six runs go the other way. In four of them the last checkpoint
wins on at least one axis and loses on none. The clearest case is
nocolorada, where training for 120 more epochs after the KID minimum
improved FID from 10.20 to 8.93 and recall from 0.211 to 0.353 at the same
time. In two runs the axes disagree: for r1gamma5 the best checkpoint has the
better FID (13.62 against 14.47), but the last has higher recall (0.130
against 0.103) and much higher colour coverage (0.434 against 0.305). Taken
together, the minimum-KID checkpoint is a reliable guard against collapse,
but in six of sixteen runs it was not the checkpoint with the best diversity.

\subsection{Continued Training of the Headline Model}
\label{subsec:continuation}

The comparison above has only two points per run. To see what happens
between them, mirror2 was continued from epoch 1,200 to 1,500 with a
generator snapshot saved every 50 epochs, and each snapshot was evaluated
under the frozen protocol (Figure~\ref{fig:epochcurve}).

\begin{figure}[H]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/epoch_curve.pdf}
\caption{Continued training of mirror2. (a) Training KID. (b--e) FID,
precision, recall and colour coverage of snapshots every 50 epochs. The red
point is the published checkpoint (epoch 1,195) and the shaded band is its
value plus or minus the noise floor of Table~\ref{tab:noise}.}
\label{fig:epochcurve}
\end{figure}

Training KID kept improving, from 0.001088 at epoch 1,195 to a minimum of
0.000975 at epoch 1,420. None of the suite metrics improved. FID was worse
than at epoch 1,195 beyond the noise floor at five of the six snapshots and
within noise at the sixth. Precision was lower beyond noise at all six.
Recall stayed within noise at all six, between 0.688 and 0.717. Colour
coverage fell at five of the six, by up to 0.047 at epoch 1,500. Three
hundred more epochs gave a better training KID and a slightly worse
dataset.

Part of the explanation is measurement noise in the selection score itself.
Training KID is estimated from 1,000 real and 1,000 generated images with
subsets of 100, which is much cheaper than the 5,000-image evaluation used
for the paper's tables. Over the 27 KID evaluations after epoch 1,200 of
the sharp1 run, the standard deviation was 0.00040 and the largest value was
six times the smallest. The minimum at epoch 1,555 (0.00044) was below the
final value at epoch 1,600 (0.00064) by only about half of that standard
deviation, so the choice between them was close to a coin toss.

The practical conclusion is to treat the minimum-KID rule as an early
stopping guard against collapse, not as a way to find the best checkpoint.
When the goal is a diverse dataset, saving snapshots and evaluating them
with the full suite is the safer procedure. It costs disk space and
evaluation time, but no further training. A larger KID sample during
training would make the guard itself less noisy.
