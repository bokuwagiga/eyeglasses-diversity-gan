\abstract{Synthetic images can enlarge the small product catalogues used to train eyewear vision models, but only if the generated set is as diverse as the real one. Standard measures such as the Fr\'echet inception distance (FID) do not show where a generative adversarial network (GAN) loses diversity. This study trains a StyleGAN2-based generator on 6,511 catalogue images of eyeglass frames in eighteen configurations and evaluates every generated set against the real set with a metric suite designed for this product: colour coverage, silhouette geometry, pose-aligned mirror symmetry, mirror-axis offset dispersion as a measure of pose variety, a calibrated realistic novelty rate, and structural artifact checks, all read against a measured noise floor. Diversity was lost through colour augmentation, strong discriminator regularisation, truncation, and a mirror prior, which reduced pose variety to 0.54 of the real value. Two models are put forward: one with FID 3.58 and real pose variety, and one with recall 0.705. Both cover the real colour range, whereas the official StyleGAN2-ADA reaches FID 4.70 and colour coverage 0.422 against 0.566 for real data. The lowest-KID checkpoint was not the most diverse in six of sixteen runs. The suite makes each loss visible and gives a basis for choosing generators and checkpoints for dataset generation.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Computer vision models for eyewear, such as detectors, segmentation models
and virtual try-on systems, are trained on labelled images of eyeglass
frames~\cite{giedra2025deploying,matuzevicius2024retrospective,matuzevicius2024diverse}.
Product catalogues are the natural source of such images, but a catalogue
holds only a few thousand frames, and its images are bound by third-party
rights. Synthetic images can enlarge such a collection. Generated data have
replaced or supplemented real data in face analysis~\cite{wood2021fakeit},
medical imaging~\cite{fridadar2018liver} and image
classification~\cite{besnier2020thisdataset}. Generative adversarial networks
(GANs)~\cite{goodfellow2014gan} of the StyleGAN
family~\cite{karras2019stylegan,karras2020stylegan2} remain a practical choice
when only a few thousand training images are available~\cite{karras2020ada}.

A generated dataset is useful only if it is diverse. A generator that
produces sharp images of the same few frames adds little to the catalogue it
was trained on. However, the standard evaluation does not measure this well.
The Fr\'echet inception distance (FID)~\cite{heusel2017fid} combines fidelity
and coverage in one number, and its limits are well
documented~\cite{jayasumana2024rethinkingfid,stein2023exposingflaws}.
Precision and recall~\cite{kynkaanniemi2019precision} separate the two, but
they work in a feature space trained on ImageNet and do not show which
property of an eyeglass frame is missing: its colour, its shape, its symmetry
or its pose. Previous work~\cite{shubitidze2026masks} generated frame images
together with segmentation masks and measured diversity with a spread
statistic that has no real reference value;
Section~\ref{subsec:base} shows that such a statistic can rise while the
output becomes narrower. It is therefore not known where a GAN trained on a
small product catalogue loses diversity, or how the loss can be detected.

This paper studies that question for a catalogue of 6,511 frontal images of
eyeglass frames (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). A StyleGAN2-based generator is
trained in eighteen configurations, and every generated set is compared with
the real set by a suite of measures designed for this product. The paper
makes four contributions. First, a diversity metric suite for eyeglass frames
is proposed. It contains colour coverage and silhouette geometry measured
against the real set, a pose-aligned mirror symmetry score, the dispersion of
the mirror-axis offset as a measure of pose variety, a calibrated realistic
novelty rate, and a structural artifact suite. Each new measure is validated
on synthetic input, and all results are read against a measured noise floor.
Second, the training and sampling choices that decide diversity are
identified by ablation: the strength of discriminator regularisation, colour
augmentation, rare-colour sampling, a mirror prior in the generator, and the
truncation protocol. Third, two models on the quality--diversity front are
put forward and compared with the official StyleGAN2-ADA under the same
evaluation. Fourth, it is shown that keeping the checkpoint with the lowest
kernel inception distance (KID) guards against collapse but does not select
the most diverse checkpoint. The code, the trained generators and two
generated datasets of 10,000 images each are public.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

StyleGAN~\cite{karras2019stylegan} and StyleGAN2~\cite{karras2020stylegan2}
generate images from a learned style code and are the basis of the generator
used here. With few training images the discriminator memorises the training
set, and training diverges. Adaptive discriminator augmentation
(ADA)~\cite{karras2020ada} and differentiable
augmentation~\cite{zhao2020diffaugment} address this by augmenting the
discriminator input. Other approaches regularise the
discriminator~\cite{tseng2021regularizing} or use smaller
networks~\cite{liu2021fastgan}. The $R_1$ gradient
penalty~\cite{mescheder2018converge} is the main regulariser in this family.
Its best weight depends on the dataset~\cite{karras2020ada}, and a study on
300 medical face images found it decisive at that
scale~\cite{mohanty2024rosacea}. StyleGAN2-ADA has also been adapted to other
small domain datasets~\cite{yao2025ruralroad}, and GAN-based augmentation is
used for industrial data in which defective samples are
rare~\cite{yang2023precacyclegan}. Diffusion
models~\cite{ho2020ddpm,rombach2022ldm} now give better image quality than
GANs on large datasets~\cite{dhariwal2021diffusionbeatgans}. A GAN is kept
here because the work continues an existing GAN
pipeline~\cite{shubitidze2026masks}. The question studied, where diversity is
lost and how to measure it, does not depend on the generator family, and the
metric suite applies to any generated set.

FID~\cite{heusel2017fid} and KID~\cite{binkowski2018kid} compare the
distributions of real and generated Inception features. Precision and recall
for generative
models~\cite{sajjadi2018precisionrecall,kynkaanniemi2019precision} and density
and coverage~\cite{naeem2020prdc} report fidelity and diversity separately,
and the Vendi score~\cite{friedman2023vendi} measures diversity without a
reference set. Surveys list the strengths and weaknesses of these
measures~\cite{borji2022proscons,valdebenito2023metrics}. Two weaknesses
matter for this paper. The measures depend on Inception
features~\cite{jayasumana2024rethinkingfid,stein2023exposingflaws}, and
results vary between training runs, so small differences are not
reliable~\cite{lucic2018equal}. A separate line of work asks whether
generated samples are copies of the training
data~\cite{meehan2020threesample,alaa2022faithful}. The realistic novelty
rate of Section~\ref{subsec:novelty} belongs to this line, but it counts the
samples that are far from every training image and still inside the real
manifold. Classification accuracy on generated
data~\cite{ravuri2019cas} measures usefulness directly, but it needs a
labelled downstream task.

Generative models for eyewear have mostly been applied to faces: editing
glasses on portraits~\cite{plesh2023glassesgan}, or removing them with
training data rendered from 3D models~\cite{lyu2022portrait}. Datasets for
eyeglasses detection have been built by labelling face
images~\cite{matuzevicius2024diverse,matuzevicius2024retrospective}, and
detection models have been optimised for low-power
devices~\cite{giedra2025deploying}. Catalogue-style images of the frame
alone, together with masks, were generated
in~\cite{shubitidze2026masks}. To the authors' knowledge, no previous study
measures the diversity of a generated dataset of eyeglass frames against the
catalogue it was trained on.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{subsec:data}

The training corpus consists of 6,511 frontal catalogue photographs of
eyeglass frames, the same collection used in earlier work on joint
image--mask synthesis~\cite{shubitidze2026masks}. Each source image is square,
between 1346 and 1662 pixels on a side with a median of 1463, and
shows a single unworn frame, roughly centred, against a near-uniform light
background. Images are centre-cropped
to a 2:1 aspect ratio and resized to $256 \times 512$ pixels (height $\times$
width), which follows the natural proportions of a frame viewed from the
front and avoids the padding a square canvas would demand.

Two properties of this corpus matter for what follows. The first is the
background. Because it is close to uniform, the frame silhouette can be found
by thresholding on the distance from the background colour. No learned
segmentation is needed, and the generator does not have to produce a mask.
All geometric and symmetry measures in Section~\ref{sec:metrics} use
silhouettes obtained this way. The second property is size. At 6,511 images
the corpus is small for adversarial training at this resolution, and several
of the failures reported below were caused by the discriminator memorising
the training set.

Two data-level settings are used throughout. Every training image is
flipped horizontally with probability 0.5 when it is loaded, so the generator
has to model both orientations. This differs from the flip inside ADA, which
is applied only to the discriminator's input. One epoch is one pass over the
6,511 images. Sampling is additionally weighted
by inverse frequency over a $16 \times 16$ histogram of each frame's mean
CIELAB $a^*b^*$ chromaticity, with exponent 0.5 and a weight cap of 4. The reason for this weighting and its effect are reported in
Section~\ref{subsec:ablations}.

\subsection{Base Model and Stabilisation}
\label{subsec:base}

The starting point is the conditional StyleGAN2 variant of previous work~\cite{shubitidze2026masks}, which generated an RGB image and a binary
frame mask from a single forward pass and reached an FID of 6.13. A later
revision of that model added an eight-layer mapping network, adaptive
discriminator augmentation~\cite{karras2020ada}, path-length regularisation, a
feature-matching term and KID-based checkpoint selection. It raised the
frame-symmetry score of that work (0.905 against 0.877), but its FID rose
to 17.49, and random samples from it showed more repeated frame shapes than
the earlier model produced. Under the colour-diversity score of that work it
nevertheless scored slightly higher, 0.111 against 0.106.

This last comparison shows why the earlier diversity metrics are not reused here. The earlier colour-diversity score is a mean pairwise distance
computed on the generated images only. On the real corpus it is 0.0338, so
both generators scored far above the real data, and the model with the
narrower output scored higher. A score of this kind rewards spread, but it
does not check whether the spread matches the real distribution, and a
generator can raise it by producing colours the catalogue does not contain.
The metrics of Section~\ref{sec:metrics} always compare against the real
set.

Removing the mask branch, as the present scope requires, destabilised training
badly, and three defects had to be corrected before a stable unconditional
baseline existed. The first was the output head. The generator projected to
RGB through a $\tanh$ nonlinearity, inherited from the mask-supervised model.
Approximately 86\% of the pixels in a cropped and resized catalogue
image are near-white background, so every loss term drives the large majority
of pixels toward $+1$, where $\tanh$ has vanishing gradient. It was replaced by the linear RGB head of the official StyleGAN2
generator~\cite{karras2020stylegan2}.

The second defect was in the mapping network, and it is the one with a
plausible bearing on the repeated frame shapes of the predecessor run. Eight
stacked default linear layers with LeakyReLU activations contract the signal
at every step, because standard initialisation scales weights by the inverse
square root of the fan-in without the equalized-learning-rate correction.
This was measured at initialisation on a batch of 4096 standard-normal
latents. The style codes have a standard deviation of 0.0184, against 1.0000
for the latents. Most of the loss happens early: the standard deviation is
0.583 after the first linear layer, 0.052 after the fourth, and about 0.028
after that. A style code of that
dispersion carries almost no per-image information, which leaves per-pixel
noise injection as the dominant source of variation between samples. Replacing
the stack with equalized-learning-rate linear layers~\cite{karras2018progressive}
at a learning-rate multiplier of 0.01, as in the StyleGAN mapping
network~\cite{karras2019stylegan}, raises the
style-code standard deviation to 1.0968 under the identical measurement.

The third defect was the adversarial loss. The hinge
formulation~\cite{lim2017geometric} produces no gradient once real and fake
scores separate beyond its margins. Under mask supervision the discriminator
had never built margins wide enough for this to matter; without it, the two
distributions separate within a few epochs, the discriminator loss reaches
exactly zero, and training stalls. Substituting the non-saturating logistic loss~\cite{goodfellow2014gan},
the standard choice in StyleGAN2-ADA~\cite{karras2020stylegan2,karras2020ada},
removed the stall.
With the three corrections in place training was stable; their contributions were not measured separately.

\subsection{Training Configuration}
\label{subsec:training}

All models reported here are derived from
StyleGAN2-ADA~\cite{karras2020stylegan2,karras2020ada} and implemented in stock
PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch}, with no compiled custom operators. The
generator maps a 512-dimensional latent through the eight-layer
equalized-learning-rate mapping network described above to a 512-dimensional
style code, and synthesises through six weight-demodulated stages from $8
\times 16$ to $256 \times 512$ with skip-RGB connections and a linear output
head. A self-attention module~\cite{zhang2019self} is placed at $32 \times 64$
to give the network access to long-range structure, such as the
correspondence between the left and right halves of the frame. Channel widths follow $512 \rightarrow
256 \rightarrow 256 \rightarrow 128 \rightarrow 64 \rightarrow 32 \rightarrow
16$, giving 8.50~M generator parameters; the discriminator uses $16
\rightarrow 32 \rightarrow 64 \rightarrow 128 \rightarrow 256 \rightarrow 256$
for 2.50~M parameters. Both width multipliers were varied, and the outcome is
reported in Section~\ref{subsec:ablations}.

Two variants add a mirror prior to the generator. The fixed-mirror variant
gives each position access to the horizontally flipped feature map at the
$32 \times 64$ and $64 \times 128$ stages. The mirror-axis variant does the
same about an axis that is learned for each sample instead of the fixed
image centre. The generator without either is called the plain model.

Training uses the non-saturating logistic loss with a lazy $R_1$ gradient
penalty~\cite{mescheder2018converge} at $\gamma = 5$, applied every 16 steps.
Path-length regularisation~\cite{karras2020stylegan2} uses weight 4.0 on the
same interval, with a batch of 16. The generator also receives a
feature-matching term~\cite{salimans2016improved} at weight 2.0 with random
real--fake pairing, and a VGG perceptual term~\cite{simonyan2015vgg} at
weight 0.75 in which each generated image is paired with its nearest real
image in the batch. The discriminator applies a group-wise
minibatch standard deviation layer~\cite{karras2018progressive} over groups of
four and blurs feature maps before average
pooling~\cite{zhang2019shiftinvariant}. Adaptive discriminator
augmentation uses horizontal flips, integer-pixel translation, brightness
jitter, additive noise and cutout, with the probability ceiling at 0.95; hue
and saturation augmentation is disabled, for reasons given in
Section~\ref{subsec:ablations}. Both networks use Adam~\cite{kingma2015adam} with $\beta_1 = 0$ and
$\beta_2 = 0.99$, at learning rates of $2 \times 10^{-4}$ for the generator and
$5 \times 10^{-5}$ for the discriminator. Style mixing~\cite{karras2019stylegan}
is applied with probability 0.9. An exponential moving average of the
generator weights~\cite{karras2018progressive}, with decay 0.999, is used for
all sampling and evaluation. The batch size is 16. On a single RTX~4090
with 24~GB of memory an epoch takes about three minutes (a median of 178 to 184 seconds across the runs).

\subsection{Generation Protocol}
\label{subsec:generation}

Sampling has its own design choices, independent of training. Five were
varied. The first is the truncation coefficient $\psi$. The second is layered
truncation, where the coarse and fine synthesis stages get different
coefficients. The third is whether each image comes from a single latent or
from a blend of several style codes. The fourth is truncation toward $k$
cluster centres instead of the global style mean. The fifth is rejection
sampling, either by manifold precision or by the artifact suite of
Section~\ref{subsec:artifacts}. Nine protocols built from these choices were
compared on one intermediate checkpoint, each generating 10,000 images at a
fixed seed (Section~\ref{subsec:transfer}).

The protocol selected there, called layered, was then fixed and used for
every model comparison in this paper. It uses $\psi = 1.0$ for the three
coarse stages, which control silhouette and proportions, and $\psi = 1.2$ for
the three fine stages, which control colour and texture, with a single latent
per image, 10,000 images, and generation seed 42.

\subsection{StyleGAN2-ADA Baseline}
\label{subsec:baseline}

For comparison with an established model, the official StyleGAN2 with
adaptive discriminator augmentation~\cite{karras2020stylegan2,karras2020ada}
was trained on the same images, using NVIDIA's StyleGAN3
code~\cite{karras2021aliasfree} with its StyleGAN2 configuration. The official
code trains only on square images, so each $256 \times 512$ training image
was padded to $512 \times 512$ with 128 rows at the top and bottom in its
own background colour. The generated images were cropped back to the
central $256 \times 512$ band before evaluation. Training used batch 16,
horizontal flips, and ADA with the official set of augmentations, which
includes hue and saturation changes. Two runs were trained that differ only
in the $R_1$ weight: the heuristic of the ADA paper,
$\gamma = 0.0002 \cdot 512^2 / 16 = 3.28$, and $\gamma = 10$. FID on 50,000
generated images, the official metric, was computed every 200 kimg, and the
snapshot with the lowest value was selected, as in the official procedure.
The $\gamma = 10$ run was stopped by a rule fixed before it started: stop
when the lowest FID has not improved by more than 2\% over three snapshots,
or when FID exceeds three times its lowest value. The $\gamma = 3.28$ run
was interrupted once at 1,800 kimg and resumed from its last snapshot. A few
small changes were needed to run the official code with current versions of
PyTorch and SciPy; none of them affects training or the FID computation.
Images were generated with $\psi = 1.0$, the official default, and
evaluated with exactly the same scripts and settings as the proposed models.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{subsec:reproducibility}

Bitwise reproduction of these runs is not achievable and is not claimed.
cuDNN autotuning and non-deterministic GPU kernels were not disabled. Also,
each resumed run re-seeds the random number generator instead of restoring
its state, so the batch order after a resume differs from that of an
uninterrupted run.
What is provided is the code, the configurations and the trained weights of
the two final models, with fixed seeds. In the code repository and the data
archive the plain model is named sharp1 and the mirror-axis model mirror2. Run-to-run variation under a
different training seed was not measured. This would require repeating a
full training run, roughly 60 GPU-hours for one model.

Long runs were trained in several resumed segments. The 1,600-epoch run of the plain model, for example, consists of six segments of 300, 200, 300, 357, 43 and 400
epochs, 83.9 hours in total. Epochs are reported as the number of epochs
completed; the checkpoint files store a zero-based index one lower.

\section{A Diversity Metric Suite for Eyeglass Frames}
\label{sec:metrics}

The suite follows three design rules. First, every measure is computed
from the images alone: frame silhouettes are recovered by background
thresholding, so no generated masks and no learned segmentation are
involved, and the same code evaluates real and generated sets identically.
Second, no diversity statistic is reported without its value on the real
corpus. Section~\ref{subsec:base} showed what happens otherwise: a spread
statistic with no real reference can be improved indefinitely by drifting
off-distribution, and the earlier metrics rewarded exactly that.
Third, each new metric is tested on synthetic images with a known answer
before it is used. Section~\ref{subsec:aligned_sym} shows why this matters:
a symmetry score can respond more strongly to camera pose than to the defect
it is meant to measure.

\subsection{Geometry, Colour, Feature-Space and Perceptual Measures}
\label{subsec:metric_families}

Silhouettes are extracted by taking the median colour of the border pixels
as the background, marking as foreground every pixel whose RGB distance from
it exceeds 30, and removing specks with a $3 \times 3$ morphological opening.
This extraction did not fail on any of the 6,511 real images.

Geometric diversity is described by nine values per silhouette. Two are
areas: the fraction of the image covered by the silhouette, and the fraction
covered after filling its holes, which reflects overall frame size. Two
describe the bounding box: its aspect ratio and its height relative to the
image. Mean and maximum rim thickness, from the distance transform, separate
thin wire frames from thick acetate ones. Hollowness, one minus the ratio of
silhouette area to filled area, measures how open the lenses are. The last
two are the number of enclosed openings, which approximates the lens count,
and solidity, the filled area divided by its convex hull. For each descriptor the generated
set is compared with the real set twice: as a dispersion ratio (standard
deviations, supported by the 5th-to-95th percentile range) and as a
Jensen--Shannon distance between the two histograms. The ratio asks
whether the generator spans as much of the range as the catalogue; the
distance asks whether it occupies that range in the same proportions.

Colour is measured on frame pixels only, in CIELAB. Three statistics are
reported. The mean pairwise distance between per-image mean frame colours
measures palette spread. The entropy of a chroma-weighted hue histogram
over chromatic pixels, normalised to $[0, 1]$, distinguishes broad hue
coverage from a few well-separated colour clusters, a pattern that the
pairwise distance alone cannot see. Colour-gamut coverage
(\emph{ab~coverage}) is the fraction of occupied cells in a $16 \times 16$
histogram of per-image mean colour over $a^*, b^* \in [-40, 40]$.
Occupancy counts grow with sample size, so this quantity is only ever
compared between sets of equal size; all values in this paper use matched
5,000-image subsamples. The bin count is a free parameter; the
ordering of the sets compared in this paper is unchanged at
$12 \times 12$, $16 \times 16$ and $20 \times 20$ resolution. A generated
set can exceed the real coverage by placing frame colours in cells where
the catalogue has no examples; ab~coverage is therefore read as a
distance to the real value, not as a score to maximise.

Feature-space measures use Inception-v3 pool3
embeddings~\cite{szegedy2016inception}. FID~\cite{heusel2017fid} and KID~\cite{binkowski2018kid} (subset size 1,000)
are computed on the same 5,000-image subsamples and serve as quality
references. They are comparable between the experiments in this paper, but
not with values reported on other datasets. Improved
precision and recall~\cite{kynkaanniemi2019precision} and density and
coverage~\cite{naeem2020prdc} are computed with $k = 5$ nearest
neighbours; recall and coverage are the primary distribution-level
diversity measures. The Vendi score~\cite{friedman2023vendi} is computed
on 2,000-image subsets; because Inception features spread very little on
a single narrow product category, its absolute value is small even for
the real set, and only the generated-to-real ratio is reported. A
memorisation check compares the mean distance from each generated image
to its nearest real neighbour with the same statistic between real images;
a ratio well below one flags copying. Finally, perceptual diversity is the mean pairwise
LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018lpips}, VGG variant over 500 random pairs, within the
generated set, reported against the same statistic within the real set.

\subsection{Pose-Aligned Mirror Symmetry}
\label{subsec:aligned_sym}

Eyeglass frames are bilaterally symmetric objects, so a symmetry defect is
a structural failure worth measuring. The obvious construction, and the
one used first, mirrors the silhouette's boundary band about
the vertical centre line of its bounding box and scores the overlap. That
axis, however, is the frame's true symmetry axis only when the photograph
is perfectly fronto-parallel, and catalogue photographs are not: measured
on the real set, 30\% of images need an axis correction of three or
more pixels. The fixed-axis score therefore conflates photographic pose
with shape.

The pose-aligned version searches over horizontal axis shifts of up to
$\pm 8$ pixels and rotations of up to $\pm 3$ degrees, and keeps the best
boundary-band intersection-over-union. Table~\ref{tab:symval} in Appendix~\ref{app:runs} shows the validation on
synthetic silhouettes with known ground truth. A tilt of two degrees costs a perfectly symmetric frame
0.64 of the fixed-axis score but only 0.007 of the aligned score, while a
genuine asymmetry, one temple foreshortened by a quarter, costs 0.57 fixed
and 0.40 aligned. Alignment removes 98.9\% of the pose penalty and
retains 69.8\% of the shape penalty: the aligned score responds to
shape, the fixed score mostly to pose. The search costs about 29~ms per
image on a desktop CPU, roughly five minutes for a 10,000-image set. The silhouette is zero-padded before the search, so frames that reach
the image edge, as most catalogue frames do, are measured without bias.


\subsection{Mirror-Axis Offset Dispersion}
\label{subsec:yaw}

The axis search of the previous subsection also gives a diversity measure:
the offset of the best mirror axis from the bounding-box centre. Table~\ref{tab:symval}
establishes what it responds to. Moving a symmetric silhouette by $+12$ or $-20$ pixels leaves the offset at
exactly zero, because the search is centred on the bounding box. The offset
therefore does not measure where the frame sits in the image. What
moves it is differential temple foreshortening: shortening one temple by
10\% shifts the recovered axis by 5 pixels, by 25\% by 8
pixels. This is what a small rotation of the frame about the vertical axis (yaw)
does to a frontal photograph, so the offset measures the variety of viewing
angles the generator reproduces. The two measures complement each other: at 10\% foreshortening the aligned score barely moves
(0.973 against 0.986 for the symmetric case) while the offset already
reads 5 pixels, so mild yaw is nearly invisible to the symmetry score and
plainly visible to the offset.

The set-level statistic reports the standard deviation and interquartile
range of the offset, the fraction of images with an offset of three or
more pixels, and the ratio of the generated standard deviation to the
real one. A ratio well below one means the generator pins every frame into a canonically frontal pose. FID, KID, LPIPS and
ab~coverage do not measure pose, so they cannot detect this collapse.
Figure~\ref{fig:symaxis} shows the axis on two real images and the offset
distributions of the real set and of three generated sets.

\subsection{Realistic Novelty Rate}
\label{subsec:novelty}

Recall and coverage measure how much of the catalogue the generator
reproduces. For dataset augmentation the complementary question matters as
much: does the generator produce frames that exist nowhere in the
catalogue, and are those frames plausible designs rather than artifacts?
The suite answers it with a calibrated nearest-neighbour construction in
the same Inception feature space as the other measures. The reference set
contains every real image together with its horizontal mirror, since
training used horizontal flips and a mirrored copy of a catalogue frame is
not a new design. The novelty threshold is set from the real set. For each real image the
distance to its nearest other reference image is computed, excluding its own
mirror. The threshold $\tau$ is the 95th percentile of these distances, so
only 5\% of real images are this far from the rest of the catalogue. A generated
image is novel if its nearest-reference distance exceeds $\tau$, and
realistic if it additionally lies inside the $k$-nearest-neighbour feature
manifold of the reference set, the improved-precision
criterion~\cite{kynkaanniemi2019precision}. The realistic novelty rate is
the fraction of generated images satisfying both.

Two checks calibrate the construction. Feeding the real subsample back in
as the generated set yields a novelty rate of exactly zero, so copies can
never count as novel. Scoring one disjoint half of the real set against
the other, where exchangeability predicts a rate near the designed 5\%, yields 4.4\%. One caveat concerns comparability: the realism
criterion is evaluated against the flip-augmented reference, whose denser
$k$-nearest-neighbour manifold is stricter than the precision reported in
the main results, and the two numbers are not comparable.

The manifold test is weakest where novelty is highest. A
$k$-nearest-neighbour ball is larger where real samples are sparse, so the
realism test is most permissive in exactly the sparse regions where novel
samples are found. For this corpus these are mostly rimless and thin-wire
designs, which are also the hardest frames to render. The suite therefore
also reports a strict rate: the fraction of generated images that are novel,
inside the manifold, and pass every check of the artifact suite of
Section~\ref{subsec:artifacts}. The script also saves each novel image next
to its nearest catalogue image, so the results can be checked visually.

\subsection{Structural Artifact Suite}
\label{subsec:artifacts}

Distribution-level metrics do not show whether individual images have
visible defects. The suite therefore includes twelve per-image checks: silhouette fragment count, enclosed-opening count, silhouette
mirror overlap, lens-shape mismatch, speckle fraction, interior sharpness,
rim breaks, rim contrast, contour wobble, the fixed-axis and pose-aligned
edge-symmetry scores, and an appearance-symmetry check, the mean CIELAB
difference between frame pixels and their mirrored counterparts. Each check uses the 2nd or 98th percentile of the real corpus as its
threshold, so about 2\% of real images fail any single check. A generated
set is described by its flag rate for each check, compared with the real
rate, and by its clean rate, the fraction of images with no flags.

Two caveats govern interpretation. The checks are one-sided: they flag
too little sharpness or too many fragments, but nothing flags an image
for being implausibly clean, so a generator that collapses onto safe,
canonical configurations can score better than real. A clean rate above
the real corpus is treated in this paper as a warning sign to be
investigated, not as a success. And the checks inherit the limits of
silhouette extraction: on thin-wire, rimless and translucent frames the
thresholded silhouette is itself unstable, so part of any flag-rate gap
on those classes is measurement noise rather than generation failure.

\subsection{Training Ablations}
\label{subsec:ablations}

Eighteen configurations of the proposed generator were trained.
Table~\ref{tab:runs} in Appendix~\ref{app:runs} lists every run with its
change and its outcome, and runs are referred to below by their number in
that table. Its FID column comes from a single evaluation sweep under the
frozen protocol, so the values are mutually comparable.

Runs 1 to 3 vary the $R_1$ weight. At $\gamma = 2$ the discriminator
overfits, and training KID reaches its minimum at epoch 115. At $\gamma = 5$
the minimum is almost three times lower and comes at epoch 190. At
$\gamma = 10$ training is stable but slow: the minimum comes only at epoch
370 and is more than twice the $\gamma = 5$ value. The strongest
regularisation also gave the least diverse model. Against $\gamma = 5$ it
loses on FID (22.20 versus 13.62), recall (0.029 versus 0.104), colour
coverage (0.211 versus 0.305) and coverage (0.451 versus 0.690), every gap
far beyond the noise floor.

The colour-coverage gap of the mid-project models was a rare-mode problem:
the missing $a^*b^*$ histogram cells contained only 153 of the 6,511 real
images (2.35\%), dominated by purple and violet acetate frames.
Inverse-frequency oversampling (Section~\ref{subsec:data}), which makes
boosted images 3.7\% of training draws, raised colour coverage from 0.422 to
0.566 while FID improved from 8.90 to 7.80 (runs 7 and 9). The two runs
differ only in the sampling weights, although their selected checkpoints are
from epochs 310 and 460. A stronger version, a fixed $\times 8$ or
$\times 4$ weight on images in the missing and under-represented cells
(run 8), overshot to 0.684, above the real value of 0.566, at FID 18.60.
Run 8 also used a lower ADA probability ceiling (0.85 instead of 0.95), so
its FID is not a clean measurement of the oversampling strength alone. The
coverage overshoot does not depend on that.

Hue and saturation augmentation, the colour part of ADA, reduced colour
diversity. With it, run 5 reached colour coverage 0.168; without it, run 6
reached 0.367 at the same 400-epoch budget. The comparison was repeated later
by accident. Run 14 was launched with colour augmentation re-enabled and
reached 0.176, and run 15, identical apart from the restored setting and 200
further epochs, reached 0.613. Under run 14, 111 histogram cells holding 928
real images (14.25\%) were missing, all of them at a chroma of 7.9 or more,
while the near-neutral cells were all covered. Under run 15 the misses fell
to 9 cells holding 10 images (0.15\%). In run 14 the augmentation probability
stayed at its upper limit, so hue jitter was applied at almost every step.

Blurring the discriminator feature maps before pooling, used from run 14
onward, changed how long runs behave. Without it, the mean discriminator
margin of run 9, the difference between its mean outputs on real and on
generated images, grew from 4.5 to 37.5 over epochs 400 to 600. Over the same
period its training KID became 25 times worse (0.0021 at epoch 460, 0.052 at
epoch 595), and the recall of its final checkpoint fell to zero. With the
blur, the margin of run 15 over the same windows grew only from 1.9 to 4.4.
It kept growing slowly and reached 8.1 over epochs 751 to 800, but training
KID kept improving to the end of the run (0.0014 at epoch 745). The blur was
introduced together with horizontal flips and the group minibatch standard
deviation layer, so this comparison does not isolate it.

Up to run 15, training images were resized with a bilinear filter, while the
evaluation resized the real images with a bicubic filter. The generated
images were therefore softer than the real images they were scored against:
6.22\% of the images of run 15 failed the sharpness check of the artifact
suite, against 2.01\% of real images. Run 16 repeats run 15 with bicubic
resizing in training. After the same 800 epochs its sharpness flag rate was
2.23\% and its FID 4.50 instead of 5.03. Run 16, continued to 1,600 epochs,
is the plain model.

Plain training leaves a small symmetry gap: the pose-aligned symmetry median
of the plain model is 0.059 below the real value. The fixed-mirror variant
(run 17) closes the gap and overshoots it (0.016 above real), but it loses
diversity in two ways. Its colour coverage drops to 0.523, against 0.629 for
the plain model at the same training length. Its axis-offset dispersion falls
to 0.61 of the real value: only 9.8\% of its frames have an axis offset of 3
pixels or more, against 29.9\% of real frames. Its appearance-symmetry flag
rate of 0.30\%, against 2.01\% for real images, is better than real, which
Section~\ref{subsec:artifacts} treats as a warning sign. The mirror-axis
model (run 18) recovers most of this. It reaches recall 0.705, the highest in
this study, and colour coverage 0.613. Its aligned symmetry gap is only
$-0.017$, and its appearance-symmetry flag rate of 1.06\% is closer to the
real value. It does not recover pose variety: its dispersion ratio is 0.54,
against 1.09 for the plain model (Figure~\ref{fig:symaxis}c,d).

Four runs gave no improvement. A Sobel edge channel added to the
discriminator input (run 10) left FID unchanged (7.868 against 7.869 for
run 9). Doubling the width of both networks (run 11) diverged. Doubling the
generator with a 1.5 times wider discriminator (run 12) trained, but its FID
of 9.00 was worse than that of run 9. Applying five changes from the official
StyleGAN2 at once (run 13) ended in mode collapse.

pulls every sample toward the population mean. The blended scheme was
nevertheless the default inherited from the previous work. Layered truncation
gives the best compromise: the lowest FID at full yield, and higher colour
coverage than the filtered $\psi = 1.2$ set, because the stages that control
colour are not truncated.

Two choices brought no benefit. Truncation toward eight centres differs from
the single-centre layered protocol by 0.02 FID, which is within the noise
floor. A precision filter added to the layered protocol raised precision but
lowered recall and colour coverage, so it moved along the trade-off and did
not improve it.

\subsection{Rejection Filtering}
\label{subsec:filtering}

Post-hoc rejection of low-quality samples is an established way to trade
yield for quality~\cite{azadi2019drs}. Here the artifact suite provides the
rejection rule. This was tested on run 9, the last model that needed it, with
three settings: no filter, at most one flag, and zero flags. FID went from
7.802 to 7.956 to 8.566, precision from 0.881 to 0.892 to 0.890, and colour
coverage from 0.566 to 0.547 to 0.520. Filtering therefore moved the model
along the trade-off and did not improve it. From run 15 onward the unfiltered
clean rate is at the level of the real corpus (85.3\% against 86.1\%). The
final models use no filtering, and all their results are measured on
unfiltered output.

also gives the best dataset was therefore tested in two ways.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The question posed in the Introduction was where a GAN trained on a small
product catalogue loses diversity, and how the loss can be detected. The
ablations point to three places. The first is the balance between generator
and discriminator. Diversity did not come from weakening the discriminator:
the strongest $R_1$ penalty gave the least diverse model. It did not come
from an unconstrained discriminator either. The runs without feature-map
blur lost all recall once the discriminator margin grew, which is read here
as the discriminator memorising the 6,511 real images. The blur is the likely
cause of the improvement, because of the three changes introduced with it, it
is the only one that limits the high-frequency detail available to the
discriminator. The official StyleGAN2-ADA behaved in the same way. One change
of $\gamma$ separated a collapsing run from a strong one, in line with
reports that this weight is decisive on small
datasets~\cite{karras2020ada,mohanty2024rosacea}.

The second place is colour. Hue and saturation augmentation removed the
chromatic frames from the output and kept the near-neutral ones. This is
consistent with the leaking of augmentations described for
ADA~\cite{karras2020ada}: when a colour augmentation is applied at almost
every step, the discriminator can no longer tell which colours are real and
stops constraining the colour distribution of the generator. The same
explanation fits the baseline, whose official augmentation set includes hue
and saturation changes and whose colour coverage is the lowest of the
compared models. Rare colours also needed help from the sampler, because
2.35\% of the catalogue held all the missing colour cells. Rejection
filtering worked against this. Each stricter filter lowered colour coverage,
which suggests that the flagged images are concentrated among the rare
colours.

The third place is pose. A mirror prior in the generator brought the symmetry
of the frames close to the real value and gave the highest recall, but it
reduced the variety of viewing angles to about half of the real value.
Learning the axis for each sample did not restore it. Both variants force the
two halves of the frame to agree, which rules out the unequal temple lengths
that a slightly rotated frame shows. The loss therefore appears to be a
property of the mirror prior itself and not of the choice of axis. For a
dataset meant for training detectors or segmentation models, the plain model
is the safer choice when pose variety matters, and the mirror-axis model when
coverage of frame designs matters.

None of these losses was visible in the standard measures. FID, KID and LPIPS
do not respond to pose, LPIPS was the same for all final models, and the best
FID belonged to a checkpoint whose recall and colour coverage had already
declined. This agrees with the criticism of single-number
evaluation~\cite{jayasumana2024rethinkingfid,stein2023exposingflaws} and
supports measures that are tied to the product and always compared with the
real set. The novelty results add a caution in the other direction. All
generators, including a baseline with very low recall, produced realistic
novel images at about the rate of held-out real images, between 2 and 3\%. A
GAN trained on a catalogue therefore mostly fills the space between known
designs, and the size of a generated set should not be read as a number of
new designs.

For checkpoint selection, the minimum-KID rule should be treated as an
early-stopping guard against collapse and not as a way to find the best
checkpoint. When the goal is a diverse dataset, saving snapshots and
evaluating them with the full suite is the safer procedure. It costs disk
space and evaluation time but no further training, and a larger KID sample
during training would make the guard itself less noisy. Two practical lessons
came from failures. The resize mismatch lowered the measured sharpness of
every model for fifteen runs and was not affected by changes of network width
or of the discriminator; a deficit that does not react to any architectural
change should be looked for in the data path. In addition, two runs failed
silently. In run 10 the generator stopped updating for its last 20 epochs
without any error message, and the checkpoint of run 13 still loads without
error but no longer reproduces its evaluation after a later code change. Both
were found only because evaluation results were compared between checkpoints.

The study has several limits. It uses one dataset of one product category,
and the silhouette extraction and the metric thresholds assume a near-uniform
background. Every configuration was trained once. Variation between training
seeds was not measured, because one run costs about 60 GPU-hours, and the
noise floor covers generation seeds only. Differences between configurations
that are close to the floor may therefore not repeat~\cite{lucic2018equal}.
Several ablations changed more than one factor, as noted where they are
reported. The baseline was trained on padded square images and tuned over two
values of one parameter, so the comparison shows what the official model
gives with limited tuning and not its best possible result. The feature-space
measures rely on Inception features trained on ImageNet, and the realism test
of the novelty rate is weakest for rimless and thin-wire frames, where the
silhouettes are also unstable. Finally, the generated datasets were not
tested in a downstream task. Whether the measured gains in diversity improve
a detector or a segmentation model~\cite{ravuri2019cas} is left for future
work, together with segmentation masks for the diverse models and a mirror
prior that keeps pose as a free variable.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This paper examined how diverse a GAN-generated dataset of eyeglass frame
images is in comparison with the 6,511-image catalogue it was trained on. A
metric suite for this product was proposed and validated, and eighteen
training configurations and nine sampling protocols were compared with it.
Diversity was lost in specific places: under too weak or too strong
discriminator regularisation, under colour augmentation, under a mirror
prior, under truncation below $\psi = 1$, and in longer training after recall
had peaked. Each loss was measurable with a product-specific measure and was
not visible in FID alone. Two generators were put forward. The plain model
reaches FID 3.58 with the pose variety of the real set, and the mirror-axis
model reaches recall 0.705 with near-real symmetry but about half of the
real pose variety. Both cover the colour range of the catalogue, whereas the
official StyleGAN2-ADA at its better setting covers 0.422 of the colour cells
against 0.566 for the real set. Realistic novel designs make up about 2\% of
the generated images, the same order as for held-out real images. The
lowest-KID checkpoint protected against collapse but was not the most diverse
checkpoint in six of sixteen runs, so checkpoints for dataset generation
should be chosen with the full suite. The code, the generators and the
generated datasets are public.
